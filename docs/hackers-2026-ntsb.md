# Hackers 2026: NTSB accident and incident history of flight-school aircraft

Generated by `research/FlightSchoolMapping/scripts/63_ntsb_history.py --registry 2026-10-23 --release v0.3-20261023`. Regenerate rather than edit.

**Headline:** 1,825 of the 11,713 registered aircraft on flight-school fleet lists (15.6%) have an NTSB accident or incident record that is the same airframe, under the N-number they carry today; 1,806 (15.4%) have at least one accident. For non-school aircraft of the same make/model and build years that flew in the last year, the rate is 11.8% (any record) and 11.6% (accident): school aircraft carry about 1.32x the history of comparable active aircraft that are not on a school line. Against all registered aircraft of the same mix, flying or not, it is 13.1%.

**Most of that history comes with the airframe.** Of the 2,127 matched records on school aircraft, 1,631 (76.7%) are dated before the current owner's registration certificate was issued. Counting only records dated on or after it, 450 school aircraft (3.8%) have one, against an expected 1.8% for active comparable aircraft; the median current registration dates from 2021 for school aircraft and 2021 for the comparison.

**Control:** with FAA serial numbers shuffled among aircraft of the same make/model, the serial test matches 0 of 11,657 school aircraft (real: 1,372). The N-number alone would have matched 959 more school aircraft that the airframe test rejects.

ADS-B release v0.3-20261023, window 2025-10-23 to 2026-10-22: 171,123 registered aircraft airborne on at least one day.

**School tails that did not fly.** 1,823 school-listed aircraft were not airborne in the ADS-B window; 430 of them (23.6%) have a matched record, against 14.1% of those that flew, and 25 (1.4%) a record dated 2024-10-23 or later, against 1.1%, so recent accidents do not explain the gap. Their older mix explains part of it: standardized to their make/model and build years, all registered non-school aircraft would have 15.7% and airborne ones 14.1%. The rest is not explained here; a website that still lists aircraft the school no longer flies puts them in this group.

**Why school aircraft are higher.** They fly more: the median school aircraft airborne in the window logged 181 hours of ADS-B flight time, against 21 for airborne non-school aircraft of the same make/model codes. An airframe's chance of ever appearing in the NTSB file grows with the hours it has flown, and the comparison does not control for lifetime hours, which no public file gives. Read the ratio as "school airframes have more history", not "schools are less safe".

The certificate issue date is a proxy for when the current owner registered the aircraft. Renewals do not reset it: 89,300 valid registrations carry an issue date before 2016, which they could not under the three-year expiry in force until 2023 if a renewal re-issued the date. A school that bought an aircraft through a holding company, or re-registered after a name change, has a later date than its real start. As a cross-check where the NTSB record names an owner, the owner's name (normalized) equals the current registrant on 356 of 1,354 records (26.3%); PRE1982 and many older records have no owner field.

## The airframe test

A record counts only if the N-number agrees and either the serial numbers agree (letters and digits only, leading zeros dropped) or, where either side lacks a serial, the makes and models agree and the event year is on or after the FAA build year. Matched school records by path: make/model 565, serial 1,562. The make/model path is the weaker one: it accepts a different airframe of the same model that held the N-number earlier, and the shuffle control does not test it.

78,192 NTSB aircraft records share an N-number with a valid registration today. The test rejected 36,465 of those pairs; 28,189 registered aircraft (959 on school lists) would have had a record on the N-number alone and have none after the test. Rejected pairs by reason:

| Reason | School aircraft | All registered |
|---|---|---|
| serial differs | 434 | 13,755 |
| serial near-miss | 60 | 872 |
| no serial; make differs | 548 | 15,464 |
| no serial; model differs | 152 | 2,442 |
| no serial; event before the build year | 25 | 145 |
| no serial; build or event year unknown | 105 | 3,787 |

"Serial near-miss" pairs (one character apart, or one serial the tail of the other, with the makes agreeing) are mostly the same airframe written differently: read by hand, the school near-misses are largely Cessna serials with the model prefix dropped or 172S written as 1725, and Pipers with a stray zero. They are rejected, as the rule says; counting them would move the school rate to 15.9% and the airborne comparison to 12.2% (results table, last rows). Records filed under an earlier N-number of the same airframe are not searched, which also undercounts.

## Caveats

- **Lifetime exposure.** No public file gives an airframe's lifetime hours, so the comparison standardizes for model and age, and for flying at all in the last year, but not for how much each airframe has flown over its life.
- **Lower bounds.** Records under a previous N-number, serial typos and owners' failure to report are all missed.
- **Fleet lists.** A tail on a school's website is a claim that it is on the line; `docs/hackers-2026-scrape-facts.md` measures how well those claims hold. Tails that are not validly registered today are not in the cohort.
- **Privacy.** This doc carries aggregate counts only. The matched school tails (`school_matches.tsv`) and the rejected pairs stay in the git-ignored data directory. NTSB narratives were not exported or read.
